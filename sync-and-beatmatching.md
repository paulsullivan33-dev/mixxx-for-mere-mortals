# Sync and Beatmatching

Two songs playing together clash
unless their beats land together.
Aligning them — **beatmatching** —
is the founding skill of DJing, and
the one Mixxx's Sync button is famous
for doing for you. This chapter is
about what the button really does,
what it quietly relies on, and why
the craft's practitioners trust it
only so far. The mechanics are from
the Mixxx manual; the beat grids it
depends on are the lab's own,
measured in chapter 6.

## The manual way, briefly

By hand: play track B in your
headphones while A plays out. If B's
beats drift later than A's, B is
slower — nudge its tempo slider up;
if they drift earlier, down. Tap the
spinny forward or back to shove B's
beats onto A's, then keep listening,
because no two recordings hold
perfectly steady unless a computer
made them. It is a learnable,
satisfying skill — like driving a
manual gearbox, and with the same
relationship to the automatic that
follows.

## What Sync does

Press **SYNC** on a deck and Mixxx
sets that deck's tempo to match the
other playing deck, using the two
beat grids. Hold it (or engage sync
lock, per the preferences) and it
keeps matching as you move the other
deck's tempo. It also aligns the
grids' phase when you press play
with quantize on: the new track
starts *on* a beat, not merely at
the right speed.

Everything Sync knows comes from
analysis: BPM, first-beat position,
grid. Which means chapter 6's trial
is the whole story of when Sync
works:

- Steady, machine-made tempos —
  measured exactly, gridded exactly.
  Sync is flawless, and faintly
  boring.
- A wrong grid — the test tone's
  invented 86.25, a live drummer's
  wandering tempo, a ÷2 error — Sync
  will faithfully, confidently line
  up nonsense. The button has no
  doubt and no ears.
- Drifting sources — a rip from
  vinyl, a live recording — no fixed
  grid exists, so no fixed answer
  can. This is where the manual
  skill stops being nostalgia and
  becomes the only tool.

## Why DJs distrust it (a little)

Not because it fails often, but
because of *how* it fails: silently,
mid-set, in the blend that matters.
The professional posture is to use
Sync as an assistant, not an
authority — glance at the grids,
tap the tempo yourself when they
disagree with your feet, and keep
the hand skill alive by practising
without it. Mixxx supports the
posture: the grids are editable
(*Adjust BPM* — set the first beat
by eye on the waveform, halve or
double the tempo, tap along), Sync
is per-deck and instant to drop,
and nothing in the program hides
the raw controls behind it.

## Key, the other half of blending

Sync solves rhythm; it says nothing
about harmony. Two tracks at the
same tempo can still sound dreadful
together if their keys fight — which
is why chapter 6's key column sits
beside the BPM column, and why DJs
plan sets in compatible keys (the
Camelot wheel is the popular map of
which keys neighbour kindly). Key
lock, from chapter 7, is what lets
you change a track's tempo without
dragging its key across that map.
